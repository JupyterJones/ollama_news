The interstellar comet known as Comet ISON, officially designated as C/2012 S1 (ISON), which was the first of its kind discovered in our solar system's history. On November 28, it made an impressive close approach to Earth—just over a million miles away and within Mars’ orbit! Even though this comet could have entered interstellar space after passing by Jupiter, astronomers like Emily Leveillé-Brown of the University of Hawaii at Manoa's Institute for Astronomy (IfA) were eager to study its unique features.

The Hubble Space Telescope had previously captured Comet ISON on October 20, and initial estimates suggested a diameter roughly comparable to Greenland’s landmass—about 14 miles across! But as our understanding of interstellar objects grows with missions like Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer (WISE) in NASA's Astrophysics Next Generation Space Telescope program, we can refine these estimates.

Comet ISON is quite active: it has a large icy core and releases gas as the Sun warms its nucleus—traits of comets rather than asteroids. While Comet ISON’s size remains uncertain until further observations are made with infrared telescopes, astronomers anticipate that they could be studying this interstellar guest for years to come because it will likely stay within the solar system's ecliptic plane and return in about a century—a rare opportunity indeed!

Astronomer Emily Leveillé-Brown has been at the forefront of monitoring Comet ISON, employing powerful ground telescopes like ATLAS to detect it as early as possible. Her work exemplifies why NASA invests so much in both professional and amateur astronomy programs: discoveries such as this comet challenge our understanding of solar system boundaries and open new windows for research into the origins of these interstellar visitors!

As Comet ISON continues on its journey through space, it will provide a treasure trove of data. From studying how they fragment when nearing stars to observing their interactions with planets' atmospheres—like Jupiter and Saturn did recently (though much closer than the Earth-Sun distance), we may uncover secrets about comet composition that remain elusive in traditional solar system objects!

So, while Comet ISON might not have a direct threat to our world given its safe passage through space without any risk of impact or collision with planets like Mercury—the closest approach will still be well above Earth's orbit. As we learn more about this interstellar voyager and others in the future (and potentially discover another one yet!), there’s no doubt that Comet ISON, named for its stellar finder Isaac Newton Telescope survey team back on September 29, will inspire new questions while expanding our knowledge of these cosmic wanderers.

And remember: even as we marvel at the comet's beauty and scientific value tonight or tomorrow night sky-gazing session—as a celest0ial tourist myself—Comet ISON might just be an emblematic reminder that our solar system isn’t isolated, but is part of something much larger: interstellar space!
